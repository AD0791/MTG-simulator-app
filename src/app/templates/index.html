Where the plan stops

This tool answers one question. If you follow a staking plan and keep losing, how far can you go before the account can no longer cover the next required stake? That breaking point is the wall, and finding it is the whole purpose of the tool.

The payout mechanic

On a binary-option-style trade, two things can happen. The prediction is right and a fixed payout is returned, or it is wrong and the stake is lost. The payout is a proportion of the stake, and it is below 100%.

At a 92% payout, a winning $100 stake returns $92 profit. A losing $100 stake costs the full $100. Winning and losing are not symmetrical, and that asymmetry is where the wall comes from.

Payout ratio
The proportion of a stake returned as profit on a win. 0.92 means 92%. Payout ratios vary by asset.
Recovery stake
The amount that, if it won, would recover every loss in the run so far.
The wall
The point at which the next required stake exceeds the money remaining, so the plan cannot continue.

Why recovery staking meets a wall

To recover a run of losses with a single win, the win must return at least what the run cost. Since a win returns only a fraction of the stake, the stake has to be larger than the losses it is meant to erase — divided by the payout ratio:

stake = ⌈(cumulative loss + target profit) ÷ payout ratio⌉ stake equals the ceiling of cumulative loss plus target profit, divided by the payout ratio

At a 92% payout, that division multiplies the required stake by about 1.087 each time, on top of a cumulative loss that is itself growing. The stake does not grow with the streak; it grows with the debt the streak has already created, which is why it accelerates.

No staking pattern escapes this. Not flat, not Fibonacci, not doubling, not a debt-driven recovery formula. Below a 100% payout the arithmetic is the same for all of them; only the location of the wall changes.

The plan this tool models

  1. One or two opening entries — placed by hand on the first candle, shared by every strategy below. Two is the usual shape; the simulator also models a single opener. Neither has acted yet, so the streak has not started either way. Halving the opener does not buy extra depth into the ladder — under either strategy the plan still places the same number of entries before the wall, just with less cushion left when it arrives. A smaller opening stake is not the safer choice.
  2. From the second entry on — one of two ways of sizing the stake takes over:
Recovery formula ("adder")
stake = ⌈(cumulative loss + target profit) ÷ payout ratio⌉ stake equals the ceiling of cumulative loss plus target profit, divided by the payout ratio — the smallest whole-dollar stake that would recover the entire streak, plus any target profit, if it won. At a target of $0 this is called breakeven recovery; above $0, profit recovery.
Double
stake = 2 × previous stake stake equals twice the previous stake — the textbook martingale. Each candle doubles the one before it; on the second candle, "the one before" is both openers together. Simplest to describe, and the subject of the note below.

The simulation assumes the worst case throughout: every entry loses. It stops the instant the next required stake is larger than the balance. The simulator can run either strategy, or both side by side, against the same capital and openers.

Doubling stops recovering before it reaches the wall. A doubled stake is only ever the first-candle stake larger than everything already lost, and a win pays back the stake minus the payout's cut. So what a win leaves over is

net if won = first-candle stake − stake × (1 − payout ratio) the net if this entry wins equals the first-candle stake, minus the stake multiplied by one minus the payout ratio

The first term never grows; the second doubles every candle. At a 92% payout the cut overtakes the margin once the stake passes 12.5 times the first candle, and from there a win recovers most of the streak but still ends below starting capital. The ladder marks those entries in teal on the “If won” column. The recovery formula never does this: it rounds each stake up to whatever a full recovery takes.

Same capital, same openers, same payout — the recovery formula survives eight entries, doubling seven. Doubling's first wins pay: a win on entry 4's $80 stake would leave $1,003.60. Then the margin runs out. A win on entry 5's $160 stake leaves $997.20, $2.80 short of where the account started; on entry 6's $320 stake, $984.40, $15.60 short. And the wall arrives right after: entry 7 would need $640, and only $370 is left. Two entries before the end, doubling was already paying a win back at a loss — the whole argument for comparing the methods side by side rather than picking one on faith.

What this tool does not do

It does not estimate how likely a losing run is, because that depends on a trading strategy rather than on a staking plan. It does not predict markets. It does not claim the plan it models is profitable, and nothing here should be read as a recommendation to use it.

It reports one thing: given these numbers, the account breaks here.

Reading the results

What exactly is the wall?

It is the first entry the account cannot place. The simulator computes the stake that entry would require, compares it with the balance remaining, and stops when the stake is larger. The results page shows both numbers: what was needed, and what was left.

Why do the stakes escalate so quickly?

Each computed stake is sized to recover the entire streak so far, and the streak so far includes every previous recovery stake. The debt compounds, and dividing by a payout below 1 enlarges each step further. Growth is geometric, not linear.

What does "if it had won" mean?

It is what the balance would have been had that specific entry won instead of lost — the losses up to that point, plus the payout on that stake. From the second entry on it lands at or just above the starting capital, because that is precisely what the stake was sized to do. It is shown to make the trade-off visible: the plan aims at recovering to breakeven, and it risks the account to do it.

Why assume every trade loses?

Because the question is what the account can absorb, not what is likely. A worst case is a planning number: it holds regardless of the win rate. Mixing in a probability would produce an average that no individual losing run is obliged to respect.

Does a higher payout remove the wall?

It moves it, and does not remove it. A higher payout makes each recovery stake smaller, so the same capital survives more entries. But as long as the payout is below 100%, each recovery stake still exceeds the losses it repays, so the escalation continues and the capital is still finite. Try it in the simulator: raising the payout adds entries to the ladder and never removes the last row.

What does "share of balance" mean in the table?

The stake divided by the balance available immediately before that entry. It is shown instead of a share of starting capital because the account is already depleted by then, and a share of the original figure would understate how much is at risk.

What is target profit?

An amount demanded on top of recovery, from the second entry onward. At zero the plan aims at breakeven plus rounding. Raising it enlarges every computed stake and brings the wall closer.

Does doubling recover a loss, or not?

Only for its first few candles. Each doubled stake exceeds everything already lost by just the first-candle stake, and a win gives back the stake less the payout's cut. That cut doubles every candle while the margin stays put, so below a 100% payout it always catches up: at 92%, once the stake passes 12.5 times the first candle, a win recovers most of the streak but still ends below starting capital. Those entries are marked in teal, with the shortfall printed beside them. The recovery formula never falls short this way, because it sizes every stake to a full recovery.
